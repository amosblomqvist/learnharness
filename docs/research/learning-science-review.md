# Learning-science review of the teaching methodology

*Compiled 2026-10-27. Subject: `docs/teaching-methodology.md` (owner theory, 2026-09-24). Scope: the theory's claims only. Implementation claims are out of scope except where the doc prescribes them (Section 10).*

## How to read this document

**Verdicts:** Supported / Partially supported / Contested / Unsupported / Contradicted. "Unsupported" means I found no direct evidence either way, not that the claim is false.

**Evidence provenance.** Every cited work was confirmed to exist (author, year, venue, and usually DOI) through web search results that returned its bibliographic record. For most papers I saw only the abstract or a search-engine description of it, not the full text. Effect sizes quoted below come from abstracts or from search summaries of the paper. Where the only source I reached was secondary (a press release, blog, or aggregator), I mark the item **[secondary]** and treat it as lower confidence. Items I could not verify at all are marked **[unverified]**. Please check the primary text before quoting any number externally.

**Overall shape of the finding.** The theory's structural half (knowledge is organized, schemas matter, prior knowledge determines what can be learned, small guided steps help novices, corrective feedback matters) has strong support. Its two most distinctive epistemic claims are the weakest. These are (1) that *felt obviousness is the measure of understanding*, and (2) that *retention is explained by lock-in when a fact feels safe and connected*. The first conflicts with a large metacognition literature. The second is plausible but is stated as a mechanism with no direct evidence. The theory also omits retrieval over time and spacing, the two best-supported techniques in the literature.

## 2. Claim-by-claim evaluation

### §1: Two knowledge states

**C1. Held = accepted as obviously true.** *Partially supported.*
Prior knowledge is among the strongest predictors of what a learner can take from new material. A meta-analysis found a pooled pretest-posttest correlation of about .53 over 8,776 effect sizes (Simonsmeier et al., 2022, https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2021.1939700 [figures from a secondary summary]). The same paper reports that prior knowledge predicts final *outcomes* much better than *gains*, which is relevant to how much a probe can tell you about learning speed.
The problem is defining "held" as "feels obviously true." Learners commonly believe they hold things they cannot use, and sometimes hold confidently things that are wrong (see C3-C5 below, and the misconception literature in C14). As a definition for the *probing* phase this is risky, because self-reported or felt acceptance is a poor measure of actual prior knowledge. Metacomprehension research is poor at predicting actual performance without a performance-based cue (Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell, 2013, https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-113011-143823).

**C2. Probing can bracket both states precisely.** *Partially supported / Unsupported as stated.*
Pretesting and diagnostic assessment are well-motivated. The pretesting effect, in which attempting questions before instruction improves later learning even when the attempts fail, is replicated across several experiments (Richland, Kornell & Kao, 2009, "The pretesting effect: Do unsuccessful retrieval attempts enhance learning?", J. Exp. Psychol.: Applied 15(3), 243-257, https://learninglab.uchicago.edu/Pre-Testing_files/RichlandKornellKao.pdf; Kornell, Hays & Bjork, 2009, https://doi.org/10.1037/a0015729). Those results concern *free* benefit from testing, not a claim that a short probe can pin a knowledge state down "precisely." I found no study showing that conversational probing yields a precise or complete map of held knowledge. The illusion-of-explanatory-depth work shows people overrate what they can explain until asked to do it (Rozenblit & Keil, 2002, https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog2605_1; abstract confirmed, with a published challenge that part of the effect reflects how the rating question is read [secondary]). Probes should therefore ask the learner to *produce* (explain, predict, apply), not to say what they already know. Open question for the owner: how is "held" established, by self-report or by performance?

### §2: Learning is the goal feeling obviously true

**C3, C4. Terminal condition and measure = felt self-evidence.** *Contradicted as a sole criterion; partially supported as a necessary-ish accompaniment.*
This is the claim most at odds with the literature. Detailed assessment is in Section 3(a) below. Summary: subjective feelings of knowing and understanding are driven by fluency and ease cues that are only weakly diagnostic of later performance and are sometimes inversely related to it (Koriat & Bjork, 2005, https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.31.2.187; Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015, https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691615569000; Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell, 2013; Deslauriers et al., 2019, https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1821936116). Truth-feelings can be induced independently of evidence (Reber & Schwarz, 1999; Laukkonen et al., 2020). "Recall, answering questions, coverage" are not valid *substitutes* for understanding, and the theory is right about that. The delayed, transfer-oriented test of the goal proposition is the better criterion; felt obviousness can be a secondary signal.

**C5. Recited-but-not-self-evident facts "rot"; self-evident facts are held.** *Partially supported (direction), Unsupported (mechanism and the dichotomy).*
- *Supported:* Rote or shallow encoding is worse for retention and transfer than meaningful, organized encoding. Prior-knowledge-linked material is learned and kept better (see C9-C10).
- *Not supported:* The idea that retention is determined by whether the fact feels obvious. Forgetting is a function of retrieval history and spacing as well as meaning. Even well-understood material fades without retrieval. Spaced and repeated retrieval strongly improve retention of *meaningful* material too (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006, "Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention", Psychol. Sci. 17(3), 249-255, https://psychology.ecu.edu/wp-content/pv-uploads/sites/216/2019/03/Roediger-Karpicke-2006.pdf; Karpicke & Blunt, 2011, "Retrieval practice produces more learning than elaborative studying with concept mapping", Science 331, 772-775, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3577217; Cepeda et al., 2006, https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354). Karpicke & Blunt is particularly relevant: concept mapping is a structure-building, elaborative activity, and retrieval beat it, including on inference questions and when the test itself asked for a concept map. Structure helps, but it does not replace retrieval.
- The "memorized vs held" binary is an oversimplification. Fluent, well-practiced procedures can be highly durable without feeling "obvious" in the sense of derivability.

**C6. Retention mechanism = safe lock-in + connectedness; obviousness is the felt signal of both.** *Unsupported as stated; the "connected" half is partially supported.*
- *Connectedness:* Existing schemas speed consolidation. In rodents, a pre-existing cortical schema allowed new paired associates to become hippocampus-independent very quickly (Tse et al., 2007, "Schemas and memory consolidation", Science 316, 76-82, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2271071 [abstract/secondary description only]). Chess and physics expertise work shows organized knowledge supports better storage and retrieval (Chi, Feltovich & Glaser, 1981, "Categorization and representation of physics problems by experts and novices", Cognitive Science 5, 121-152, https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog0502_2). This is animal and expert-novice evidence, so it supports the direction but not the specific mechanism.
- *"The brain commits only when safe to lock in":* I found no literature that states or tests this as a memory-commitment mechanism. It is a hypothesis in the owner's own terms. Treat as an open conjecture, not as established. The related phenomenon of reconsolidation and interference exists, but I did not verify a primary source that matches this claim, so I do not cite one.
- *"Obviousness is the felt experience of both":* This identification is untested and runs into the fluency literature (C3-C5).

### §3: The click

**C7. Loose facts collapse into a generative structure.** *Partially supported.*
The phenomenon of restructuring and chunking into higher-order principles is well documented in expert-novice research: experts organize knowledge around deep principles, novices around surface features (Chi, Feltovich & Glaser, 1981). Insight/"aha" experiences accompanying restructuring are real and have been studied. What is not established is that learning *consists* of a particular kind of compression event, or that such events can be engineered by ordering. It is a good descriptive account of one kind of learning. Facts that are inherently arbitrary (vocabulary, dates, conventions) do not collapse into generators and must be retained by other means (retrieval, spacing).

**C8. Knowing why is the strongest form of understanding and the clearest felt evidence of structure.** *Partially supported (first half) / Contradicted (second half).*
The first half matches mainstream views: deep, principled understanding supports transfer. The second half, that the *feeling* of the click is clear evidence, is exactly what the fluency and insight work calls into question. Artificially induced "aha" experiences made unrelated false statements feel more true (Laukkonen, Kaveladze, Tangen & Schooler, 2020, "The dark side of Eureka", Cognition 196:104122; n=300, d≈.63 in the aha condition [from search description of the abstract]; PDF at https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/cognitive-bias/illusion-of-depth/2020-laukkonen.pdf). This shows that insight feelings can be dissociated from truth. The experiment is an induced-aha manipulation, not a direct test of instruction, so it is a caution rather than a refutation.

### §4: Knowledge is structure

**C9. Understanding is a graph of propositions and connections; connections are themselves facts.** *Partially supported.*
Schema theory and network models of semantic memory are mainstream, and expert knowledge is hierarchically organized around principles (Chi et al., 1981; Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas, 1998, "Cognitive architecture and instructional design", Educ. Psychol. Rev. 10(3), 251-296; updated in Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas, 2019, https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5). Two cautions. First, a proposition-and-edge graph is one representational choice among others (schemas, mental models, procedures, imagery, embodied knowledge). The literature does not establish that propositional nodes are the right unit; a lot of skilled knowledge is procedural and not well captured as "true propositions." Second, the claim that connections "are themselves facts" that can be stated and held is a useful design stance. It also matches evidence that learners often hold facts but miss the relations (Rozenblit & Keil, 2002, on explanatory depth).

**C10. Unconnected propositions are fragile; nested ones are self-preserving.** *Partially supported.* See C6 (schema-assisted consolidation; expert organization). The "self-preserving" wording overstates: nested knowledge is *easier to reconstruct and retrieve*, but still needs periodic retrieval to stay accessible (Cepeda et al., 2006; Karpicke & Blunt, 2011).

### §5: Teaching = facts in order, one reasoning step at a time

**C11. A lesson is facts in the right order presented clearly.** *Partially supported / incomplete.*
Presentation and order matter (cognitive load theory and worked-example research below). The claim is incomplete because the literature stresses what the *learner does* with the material, not only how it is presented. Chi & Wylie's ICAP framework hypothesizes that learning rises as engagement moves from passive to active to constructive to interactive, and that merely receiving well-presented material is the weakest mode (Chi & Wylie, 2014, "The ICAP framework", Educ. Psychologist 49(4), 219-243, https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2014.965823; one later lab study with 161 undergraduates found a contrary pattern, attributed partly to cognitive load [secondary]). Instructional explanations "often do not work" unless learners process them actively (Wittwer & Renkl, 2008, Educ. Psychologist 43, 49-64, https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520701756248 [existence verified; content from secondary description]). Self-explanation prompts have a mean effect of g ≈ .55 over 69 effect sizes (Bisra et al., 2018, "Inducing self-explanation: A meta-analysis", Educ. Psychol. Rev. 30, 703-725, https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2018-bisra.pdf). The theory's checks (C25) partly supply learner activity, but "the atomic act of teaching is exposing the learner to a fact" understates it.

**C12. One reasoning step at a time, each small enough to be understood at once.** *Supported for novices; Contested as a universal rule.*
- *For:* Working memory is severely limited when handling novel interacting elements; novices learn faster from worked examples than from unguided problem solving (Sweller, 1988, "Cognitive load during problem solving: Effects on learning", Cognitive Science 12(2), 257-285, https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1202_4; Sweller et al., 1998). A meta-analysis of worked examples in mathematics found g = 0.48 (Barbieri, Miller-Cotto, Clerjuste & Chawla, 2023, https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1). Step-based tutoring systems were nearly as effective as human tutors (d ≈ 0.76 vs 0.79 vs no tutoring) in VanLehn's review (VanLehn, 2011, "The relative effectiveness of human tutoring, intelligent tutoring systems, and other tutoring systems", Educ. Psychologist 46(4), 197-221, https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2011.611369 [figures from secondary summaries]). Guided, explicit instruction beat unassisted discovery (d = -0.38 for unassisted discovery vs explicit instruction in a meta-analysis of 164 studies; Alfieri et al., 2011, https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021017). Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006) argue the same on cognitive-architecture grounds (https://www.davidlewisphd.com/courses/EDD8121/readings/2006-Kirschner_et_al.pdf).
- *Against universality:* (i) Expertise reversal: guidance that helps novices can be redundant or harmful for more knowledgeable learners (Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller, 2003, Educ. Psychologist 38(1), 23-31, https://ro.uow.edu.au/edupapers/136; Kalyuga, 2007, https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-007-9054-3). Step size should *grow* as the learner's schemas grow. (ii) "Single reasoning step" is not an objective unit: what counts as one step depends on the learner's chunking, so the right granularity is learner-specific. This is the "element interactivity" problem; cognitive load theory has been criticized for lack of independent measurement of load and for circularity (see Section 3(c)). (iii) The same alignment with "assistance dilemma" work: the optimal amount of help varies and no general rule has been established (Koedinger & Aleven, 2007, https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-007-9049-0).

**C13. Each landing is self-evident because it follows from what is held.** *Partially supported / Contradicted as a guarantee.*
Small, well-prepared steps do lower load and raise comprehension. But logical derivability and felt obviousness differ. A step can "follow logically" and be accepted without being understood. A learner who feels it is obvious can be wrong (illusions of knowing; see Section 3(a)). The mere fact of an easy, fluent derivation can inflate confidence while *reducing* retention and transfer (the learning-vs-performance gap: Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015). The theory's wish that every step land as "of course" actively favors low-difficulty instruction, which sits uneasily with desirable difficulties (Section 3(b)).

**C14. Errors surface instantly with small steps; repair before continuing; never build on an unchecked step.** *Partially supported.*
- *Supported:* Immediate corrective feedback and mastery-before-advancing are effective. Mastery learning programs show positive effects on exam performance, larger for weaker students (Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns, 1990, Rev. Educ. Res. 60(2), 265-299, https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543060002265 [108 evaluations; abstract via secondary listing]). Enhanced/assisted discovery with feedback, scaffolding and elicited explanations helps (d = 0.30; Alfieri et al., 2011).
- *Qualifications:* (i) Misconceptions are often coherent mental models, not isolated errors, and are resistant to a single correction. Chi distinguishes easily corrected isolated false beliefs from flawed mental models that require model replacement rather than overwriting (Chi, 2008, "Three types of conceptual change", in Vosniadou (Ed.), *International Handbook of Research on Conceptual Change*, 61-82, https://www.public.asu.edu/~mtchi/papers/Chi_concpetualchangechapter.pdf [chapter itself not read; description is secondary]). A correct-looking answer to a small check does not rule out a latent misconception. Refutation-style instruction, which names and rebuts the misconception, has been reported to outperform plain exposition in reviews, but one source notes that this has recently been met with skepticism (I could not reach the Tippett 2010 review itself; **[unverified]**). (ii) "Never build on anything not yet held" conflicts with productive-failure and pretesting findings in which learners benefit from attempting things before holding them (Section 3(b)). (iii) Immediate checks tap short-term memory; an answer right after a step does not show it will survive a delay (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006; Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015).

### §6: Foundations

**C15. Foundational nodes should be unconditionally true: no caveats, no hedging.** *Contested.*
- *For:* Simplifying to reduce load is supported for novices, and cluttering instruction with extraneous material hurts (Sweller et al., 1998; the seductive-details meta-analysis found medium negative effects, Rey, 2012, Educ. Res. Rev. 7(3), 216-237, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2012.05.003 [figures from secondary summary]).
- *Against:* Oversimplified "true" statements are the seed of persistent misconceptions in complex domains. Cognitive flexibility theory documents "reductive" simplification as a source of later error (Spiro, Feltovich, Jacobson & Coulson, 1991, in Duffy & Jonassen (Eds.), *Constructivism and the Technology of Instruction*, Erlbaum; **[secondary]**, only descriptions and bibliographic record reached, see http://tecfa.unige.ch/themes/sa2/act-app-dos2-fic-cognitiv-flex). The theory itself notes (in the vision doc) that "truth is context-relative": "gravity is a force" and "gravity is not a force" coexist. A foundation stated without its scope ("unconditionally") can be unconditionally false outside its domain. Plausible refinement: state foundations plainly *with their scope conditions built in* ("In classical mechanics, ..."), which is not hedging.

**C16. Motivated foundations are easier to accept and hold.** *Partially supported.*
Posing a question before the answer helps: pretesting (Richland et al., 2009), and unsuccessful retrieval attempts before the answer improved later memory for the answer (Kornell, Hays & Bjork, 2009). Curiosity-based accounts, such as the information-gap view (Loewenstein, 1994, "The psychology of curiosity", Psychol. Bull. 116(1), 75-98; I only reached **secondary** summaries), predict that a perceived gap raises attention. Two cautions. First, the benefit in these studies is for *subsequent learning*, not necessarily for *acceptance* or for the feeling of self-evidence; "easier to hold as self-evident" is the owner's extension. Second, the motivating question works best if the learner makes a genuine attempt to answer it; a question that is merely read may not give the same benefit (Richland et al. control the attention-cueing alternative in Experiment 5).

### §7a: Effort allocation

**C17. Finite effort per session; optimize the proportion directed at the material.** *Partially supported.*
Cognitive load theory supports the idea that extraneous processing competes with learning for limited working memory, and that removing it helps novices (Sweller et al., 1998; 2019). Two cautions on the "finite effort per session" framing. First, the notion of a depletable *global* resource ("ego depletion") has failed large preregistered replication: pooled d = 0.04, CI [-0.07, 0.15] across 23 labs and N = 2,141 (Hagger et al., 2016, https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691616652873). Working-memory capacity *per moment* is well established; a session-level reservoir of "effort" is not. Second, cognitive load theory's own constructs are contested: de Jong and others have criticized the intrinsic/extraneous/germane framework and its measurement (see Section 3(c); I did not retrieve the primary critique, so this is **[unverified]**).

**C18, C28. The system should absorb planning, source-finding, fact-checking and ordering; the learner never does.** *Partially supported; Contested for the strong form.*
- *For:* Self-regulated study decisions are often poor. Learners rely on misleading cues when choosing what and how to study, favor what feels fluent, and under-use effective strategies (Kornell & Bjork, 2007, "The promise and perils of self-regulated study", Psychon. Bull. Rev. 14(2), 219-224, https://sites.lifesci.ucla.edu/psych-bjorklab/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Kornell_RBjork_2007.pdf; Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell, 2013). Outsourcing sequencing to a competent planner is therefore defensible.
- *Against the strong form:* Some "logistics" are desirable difficulties or generation tasks. Generating (rather than reading) improves memory (g/d ≈ 0.40, 86 studies, Bertsch et al., 2007, https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03193441). Productive failure holds that attempting to solve a problem *before* instruction, which includes figuring out what is relevant, improves conceptual understanding and transfer (Section 3(b)). Making the learner reason about what they need to learn and in what order is a form of metacognitive and planning practice with its own learning value, and it supports self-regulation as a skill. The theory therefore needs a distinction between (i) logistics that are *extraneous* (finding a reliable source, checking a fact, formatting) and (ii) planning-like decisions that are *constitutive of the learning* (what is relevant, what is the structure, what does the problem need). The doc's list lumps both ("figuring out what to learn, in what order").

**C19. Struggle with the material is how it locks in.** *Supported with boundary conditions.*
Effortful retrieval and generation produce better retention (Rowland, 2014, "The effect of testing versus restudy on retention", Psychol. Bull. 140(6), 1432-1463, https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559; Bertsch et al., 2007). This claim is *in tension* with C13 (every step lands as "of course" and is self-evident) and C14 (never build on anything not yet held). If every step is made easy and obvious, the learner may not experience the productive struggle C19 says is essential. The doc does not reconcile "struggle" and "every landing self-evident."

### §7b: The minimal tree

**C20. Minimal dependency tree; do not reteach held material; omit off-path material.** *Partially supported.*
- *For:* Pruning known material avoids the redundancy and expertise-reversal costs (Kalyuga et al., 2003). Irrelevant but interesting extras can impair learning (seductive-detail effect, Rey, 2012).
- *Against / qualifications:* (i) Prerequisite knowledge predicts outcomes but knowledge-structure topologies are not unique; there are many valid routes, and "the minimal tree" presumes a canonical derivation. (ii) Strict pruning removes spaced re-exposure and retrieval of held material, which keeps held material held (Cepeda et al., 2006; Dunlosky et al., 2013, "Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques", Psychol. Sci. Public Interest 14(1), 4-58, https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266, which rated practice testing and distributed practice highest). (iii) Contrasting cases and variation, which come from *outside* the shortest path, improve transfer and discrimination (interleaving: g = 0.42 over 59 studies, but heterogeneous, Brunmair & Richter, 2019, https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209 [from secondary summary]; Loibl, Roll & Rummel, 2017, https://www.scinapse.io/papers/2495004645 [review identifies contrasting cases as a condition for benefit]). "Nothing off the path is taught" would forgo these. (iv) "Minimal depth" is not obviously optimal: redundancy that supports multiple derivations of the same fact increases robustness and transfer.

**C21. Planning is the highest-leverage work.** *Unsupported (not tested), plausible.* I found no study isolating the share of lesson quality attributable to planning versus delivery. Task analysis and sequencing do matter (worked examples, mastery learning), but an effect-attribution claim of this kind is not directly evidenced. Note also that the more precise the plan, the more it risks the expertise-reversal and adaptivity problems above if it is not revised in response to the learner's responses.

**C22. Plan tree is a coarse map; reasoning steps are fine-grained.** *Supported in spirit (advance-organizer logic); not directly tested for this design.* Giving learners a structural overview (signaling/organizers) generally helps: a meta-analysis of signaling found overall benefit across 103 studies (Schneider, Beege, Nebel & Rey, 2018, Educ. Res. Rev. 23, 1-24, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2017.11.001; sample sizes from the abstract, effect sizes not retrieved). The doc's design stance that the map should not leak into the lesson is a design choice, not an evidence claim.

### §8: Presentation

**C23. Choose presentation mode per step for clarity.** *Supported.* Matches the broad finding that no single presentation format is best across learners and content, and that the best format depends on prior knowledge (expertise reversal; McNamara, Kintsch, Songer & Kintsch, 1996, "Are good texts always better?", Cognition and Instruction 14, 1-43 [verified via abstracts and secondary summaries]). That paper is also a caution: more coherent text helped some readers but for high-knowledge readers a *less* coherent text, which required more inference, produced deeper understanding. "Clearest possible" is not always "best for learning".

**C24. Meta-narration, labeled segments, restated structure are overhead; remove by default.** *Contested.*
- *For:* Redundant and extraneous material imposes load and can hurt (redundancy effect and seductive details; Sweller et al., 1998; Rey, 2012).
- *Against:* Signaling, which highlights organization (headings, previews, explicit structure), generally *improves* learning (Schneider et al., 2018, 103 studies; effect-size detail not retrieved, so the direction is verified but the magnitude is not). Previews and organizers are signaling devices. So "no restating of structure the learner can see" is right (restatement of visible structure is redundant), but "no meta-narration" is too strong where the structure is *not* visible or the learner is a novice who cannot infer it. This is a boundary-condition problem, not a contradiction, since the doc says "explicitness only where it serves absorption."

### §9: Checks

**C25. Every reasoning step ends with a graded check.** *Partially supported; frequency and timing are unsupported as stated.*
- *For:* Retrieval practice robustly beats restudy for delayed retention. The Adesope et al. meta-analysis (118 articles, 272 effect sizes, 15,472 participants; Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan, 2017, https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316689306 [figures from a secondary report of the abstract]) found practice tests outperform restudy and other comparison conditions, with effect sizes varying by test format and feedback. Rowland (2014) found larger benefits with feedback, with cued recall over recognition for the initial test, and with retention intervals of at least one day. Practice testing and distributed practice received the highest utility ratings in the Dunlosky et al. (2013) review. Retrieval gains also transfer to new question formats and problem-solving, though less than to identical items (Pan & Rickard, 2018, https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000151 [d ≈ 0.58 from secondary note]).
- *Qualifications:* (i) The testing effect is *delayed*; an immediate check on a step just taught is mostly a comprehension check, not retrieval practice that strengthens memory. Restudy beats testing on very immediate tests (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006). (ii) The testing effect may shrink as material complexity (element interactivity) grows, though this is contested: van Gog & Sweller (2015, https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-015-9296-4 per the Erasmus repository page; I verified the paper but only partly its DOI) argue that it decreases or vanishes for highly interactive material, while a rebuttal in the same issue (Rawson and colleagues) argues the construct is not measurable and that studies show retrieval effects with complex materials. (iii) "A check after *every* step" is a design choice with no direct evidence on optimal cadence; the doc itself flags it as a revisable locked decision. (iv) A check that can be answered by pattern-matching the immediately preceding sentence will not detect shallow understanding. Checks should require producing an inference, not echoing.

### §10 (as prescribed by the doc)

**C26. Probing precision is the ceiling on planning quality.** *Plausible, not directly tested* (see C2).

**C27. Never multi-step lectures followed by one check.** *Contested as phrased.* Small-step feedback is effective for novices (VanLehn, 2011 step-based vs answer-based tutoring: 0.76 vs 0.31 by the secondary summary I reached). But expertise reversal implies step size should adapt upward with proficiency, and fixed single-step granularity for a learner who already holds much of the structure can be redundant (Kalyuga et al., 2003; 2007).

## 3. The three focal assessments

### (a) Is "feeling obviously true" a valid learning criterion?

**Short answer: not as a stand-alone criterion, and in some conditions it is anti-correlated with actual learning. As a necessary-but-insufficient internal signal it is acceptable.**

Evidence against felt obviousness as a measure:

1. **Judgments of learning follow fluency, not learning.** Learners judge learning from cues available at study time, which are present at study but absent at test, producing foresight bias and overconfidence (Koriat & Bjork, 2005; abstract verified, full PDF at https://newiipdm.haifa.ac.il/wp-content/uploads/2015/05/2005-Koriat-Bjork-JEPLMC-KBA.pdf). The authors also report that debiasing is possible, but only when it targets theory-based judgments, and it transfers to new items only in that case (Koriat & Bjork, 2006, per search description).
2. **Performance and feeling during instruction are poor proxies for durable learning.** "Training performance" and long-term learning can be inversely related (Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015). Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell (2013) review how judgments of learning are shaped by current performance and fluency, and how this leads to poor study decisions.
3. **Easier instruction feels better and teaches less.** In randomized comparisons in introductory physics, students in actively engaged classrooms learned more but *felt* they learned less than students in fluent passive lectures; the authors attribute the gap partly to the greater cognitive effort being read as poorer learning (Deslauriers et al., 2019, PNAS 116(39), 19251-19257). The theory's design of "every landing is self-evident" is close to the fluent-lecture pole of this contrast. The study is on college physics, so generalization to one-on-one chat teaching is an inference.
4. **Illusion of explanatory depth.** People rate their understanding of mechanisms highly until they must explain them, and the illusion is stronger for explanatory (causal) knowledge than for facts, procedures or narratives (Rozenblit & Keil, 2002). This is exactly the "why" knowledge the theory targets. A published critique argues that part of the effect depends on how participants interpret the rating question [secondary: Silk-Eglit & Kurtz, 2011, CogSci proceedings, reached only via a search description]; the basic dissociation between felt and demonstrated understanding is widely replicated, but I did not verify replication details.
5. **Truth feelings are manipulable.** Easier-to-read statements are judged more true (Reber & Schwarz, 1999, Consciousness and Cognition 8(3), 338-342; abstract verified). Induced "aha" experiences raised truth ratings of unrelated false statements, with the effect strongest when an aha was reported (Laukkonen et al., 2020). This is direct evidence that "feels obviously true" is a heuristic that can be triggered by things other than evidence or derivation. Since the theory *also* tries to maximize fluent, smooth, "of course" presentation (§5, §8), its design could raise felt obviousness through exactly these channels without raising understanding.

Evidence in favor or partially in favor:
- Understanding grounded in derivation does support transfer (expert organization, Chi et al., 1981), and a derivation-backed feeling of "of course" is likely more valid than a bare feeling of familiarity. The owner's version (obviousness *because derived*) is stronger than raw fluency. But the theory defines the criterion by the feeling, and does not state an independent behavioral test; the literature says feeling is the part that is unreliable.

**Recommended revision:** keep felt self-evidence as a *teaching target and an early signal*, but make the terminal criterion behavioral: the learner can (i) re-derive the goal from held premises without prompts, (ii) apply it to a new case (transfer), (iii) explain *why* alternatives fail, and (iv) do so after a delay. This is consistent with the theory's rejection of "mere recall."

### (b) Does minimizing learner effort on logistics conflict with desirable difficulties, productive failure and the generation effect?

**Short answer: partly. The theory's *extraneous* logistics (source-finding, fact-checking) are fine to offload. Its "figuring out what to learn, in what order" is closer to the material and can be a learning activity. The doc's own stance that "struggle with the material is how it locks in" (C19) protects it from the strongest version of the objection, but the lesson design (C12-C13) undercuts that stance.**

- **Desirable difficulties.** Conditions that make initial performance harder (spacing, interleaving, retrieval, generation, variation) often improve long-term retention and transfer, and learners misjudge them as less effective (Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell, 2013; Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015). A lesson that guarantees that every step is obvious and checked at once is built against *spacing, interleaving, and generation*. These difficulties are not on the doc's list of effortful things ("following steps, answering checks, holding the new facts").
- **Boundary condition (important):** Difficulties are desirable only if the learner has the knowledge to meet them. For novices, high difficulty can overload. The research on difficulty also does not license making everything hard; the explicit claim is only that *some* difficulties raise long-term learning. Also, a review of the self-regulated-study work notes that learners tend to *avoid* effortful strategies and misread effort as poor learning (Deslauriers et al., 2019; Kirk-Johnson, Galla & Fraundorf (2019) propose a "misinterpreted-effort" hypothesis, but I could not verify that paper, **[unverified]**), so a system that *removes* learner choice about difficulty has a real argument for the learner's own sake. This is a point in the theory's favor.
- **Generation effect.** Generating information rather than reading it improves memory by about d = 0.40 (Bertsch et al., 2007). The theory's check questions provide generation of *answers*, but the plan, order and relevance judgments are not generated by the learner.
- **Productive failure (Kapur).** Learners who first attempt ill-structured problems, then receive instruction, outperform those who receive instruction first on conceptual understanding and transfer, with little cost to procedural fluency. The meta-analysis of 53 studies / 166 comparisons found problem-solving-then-instruction ahead overall; effects were weaker for young learners and absent for procedural skills (Sinha & Kapur, 2021, "When problem solving followed by instruction works", Rev. Educ. Res., https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211019105 [study counts and moderators from press coverage and a search summary; I did not read the paper]). Original studies: Kapur (2008), "Productive failure", Cognition and Instruction 26(3), 379-424 [citation verified through secondary pages only]. The conditions matter. A theory of "when and how" it works finds benefits only under specific designs (contrasting cases, building instruction on students' own solutions) (Loibl, Roll & Rummel, 2017, Educ. Psychol. Rev. 29(4), 693-715), and a conceptual replication on Bayesian reasoning found no main effect (reported in the same search result as a later study; **[secondary]**). The honest summary is: promising, not uniform.
- **Net assessment.** The owner's stated principle (offload logistics, keep struggle on the material) is defensible, and the design rationale for offloading source reliability and fact-checking is sound. The conflict arises where (i) "figuring out what to learn" is part of the structure-building (it can be what the problem-first literature calls "preparing for instruction"), and (ii) the theory's insistence on every step being obvious and checked eliminates the unsuccessful-attempt phase that pretesting and productive-failure results say is useful. A theory-consistent fix is to allow a *deliberate attempt before the step*, which also fits §6's "motivated foundations."

### (c) Is strictly one-step-at-a-time worked-example-style teaching optimal vs problem-first? What about expertise reversal?

**Short answer: Step-wise, guided teaching is well supported for novices and procedural/well-structured material. It is not supported as universally optimal. The evidence favors adapting step size and ordering to the learner's prior knowledge and the type of learning goal.**

- **For worked examples and small steps (novices).** The worked-example effect arose from Sweller's 1988 analysis that means-ends problem solving consumes capacity that schema acquisition needs. The mathematics worked-example meta-analysis (g = 0.48; Barbieri et al., 2023) shows a medium effect whether examples are used for initial acquisition or for practice. Alfieri et al. (2011) found unassisted discovery inferior (d = -0.38) and assisted discovery superior to other instruction (d = 0.30), so the real contrast is *guidance level*, not "direct instruction vs discovery." Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006) is a one-sided argument for guidance, and critics note it conflates minimal guidance with problem-based approaches that are in fact heavily scaffolded [secondary description of critiques].
- **Expertise reversal.** The advantage of worked examples and detailed guidance fades and reverses as learners gain expertise. Instructional features that help novices become redundant for experts and then consume resources (Kalyuga et al., 2003; Kalyuga, 2007). One reported follow-up found the effect on transfer but not on retention [secondary description]. Implication: a fixed policy of one step per check for *every* learner is predicted to hurt as the learner's schemas grow within a session and across sessions. The theory partly anticipates this (probe prunes what is held), but the "never multi-step" rule (C27) is stated unconditionally.
- **Problem-first / productive failure.** Problem-first followed by instruction can beat instruction-first on conceptual and transfer outcomes, particularly for older learners and conceptual (not procedural) goals (Sinha & Kapur, 2021, per the search summary; Loibl et al., 2017). Caveats: effects depend on design features, one meta-analysis summary notes direct instruction was as effective for procedural skills, and Kirschner/Sweller-camp authors dispute the generality; the debate is live, not resolved.
- **Assistance dilemma.** Koedinger & Aleven (2007) formalize that there is no known general rule for when to give versus withhold help. Fading (worked examples gradually replaced by completion problems) is the established way to implement the transition from guidance to independence (Renkl, 2014, "Toward an instructionally oriented theory of example-based learning", Cognitive Science 38(1), 1-37 [verified via abstract and citations only]).
- **Net assessment.** One-step-at-a-time is a good *default for novices on well-structured, derivable content*, which fits the theory's chosen domain of derivations. It should be (i) adaptive (larger steps for those who hold more), (ii) combined with periodic generative tasks (self-explanation, predict-then-reveal, which the self-explanation meta-analysis supports at g ≈ .55), and (iii) not presented as optimal for ill-structured domains or for conceptual-change goals, where contrasting cases and prior attempts may help.

## 4. Summary

### Strongest-supported claims
- Knowledge organization matters; connected, schema-based knowledge supports understanding and transfer (C9, C10 directionally; Chi et al., 1981; Tse et al., 2007 in animals).
- Novices learn better from guided, small-step, worked-example-style instruction than from unguided discovery (C12; Sweller 1988; Barbieri et al. 2023; Alfieri et al. 2011; VanLehn 2011).
- Prior knowledge is a major determinant of what a learner can learn, so probing and pruning are well motivated (C1 directionally, C20; Simonsmeier et al. 2022).
- Checks and feedback improve learning (C25; Adesope et al. 2017; Rowland 2014; Dunlosky et al. 2013), as do mastery-before-advancing designs (C14; Kulik et al. 1990).
- Extraneous material can hurt; presentation should be tailored (C23, part of C24; Sweller et al. 1998; Rey 2012; expertise reversal).
- Learners' own study decisions are often poor, which justifies the system owning sequencing (C18 in weak form; Kornell & Bjork 2007).

### Riskiest or contested claims
1. **C3-C5, C8, C13: felt obviousness as the criterion and measure of understanding.** Contradicted by the fluency/metacognition literature (Koriat & Bjork 2005; Soderstrom & Bjork 2015; Deslauriers et al. 2019; Rozenblit & Keil 2002; Reber & Schwarz 1999; Laukkonen et al. 2020). The design goal of making every landing smooth and obvious is the same dimension the literature shows can inflate confidence without raising learning.
2. **C5-C6: the retention mechanism ("memorized facts rot", "lock-in when safe and connected").** Direction (connected is better) is supported; the mechanism and the memorized/held dichotomy are not. Retrieval history and spacing are the main drivers of durability, and they are absent from this account.
3. **C15: foundations should be unconditionally true with no caveats.** Risk of seeding persistent misconceptions in complex domains (cognitive flexibility theory; conceptual-change work). Fixable by building scope into the statement.
4. **C20: minimal tree, nothing off-path.** Conflicts with spacing/re-exposure of held material, contrasting cases, and interleaving, all of which improve durability or transfer.
5. **C18/C28: "learner never does logistics."** Right for extraneous logistics, too strong for planning-like decisions that are part of learning (generation effect, productive failure, self-regulation skill).
6. **C12/C27: single step per check as a universal rule.** Supported for novices; expertise reversal predicts it harms as expertise grows.
7. **C17: finite per-session effort budget.** Per-moment working-memory limits are well established; a depletable session-level resource is not (ego-depletion replication failure; Hagger et al. 2016).

### Important gaps (omitted, or only deferred, by the theory)
- **Retrieval practice over time (after the session).** The theory has no mechanism for delayed retrieval. Retrieval and spacing are the two highest-utility techniques in Dunlosky et al. (2013). The vision doc already notes "sessions open with retrieval" as a spaced-repetition seed (vision.md, session design section) and lists spaced repetition under "Later" (vision.md; plan.md deferrals), but the *theory* is silent and in places (C5) implies that understanding makes this unnecessary. Spacing of 1-2 days to several weeks, scaled to the retention interval, is where the benefit is (Cepeda et al., 2006: 839 assessments in 317 experiments).
- **Delayed transfer test as the end-state criterion** (see 3(a)).
- **Desirable difficulties** (interleaving, generation, variation, spacing, pretesting) are not discussed, and the "every landing obvious" stance leans against them.
- **Learner expertise as a moving variable.** The theory has a probe at the start but no explicit adaptation of step size, guidance level or fading over the session (expertise reversal; fading).
- **Self-explanation and learner-generated elaboration** as the main active ingredient (ICAP; Bisra et al. 2018). Checks are the only learner-activity mechanism and are typed as "graded questions."
- **Misconception handling.** The theory treats errors as local gaps that surface in a check. Conceptual-change research says misconceptions are often coherent models that pass local checks and need targeted conflict/refutation and model replacement (Chi, 2008).
- **Metacognitive calibration training.** If learners judge by feel, the system will be at odds with their expectations (Deslauriers et al.); the theory does not say how to explain or calibrate this to the learner.
- **Motivation and affect.** Beyond "motivated foundations," there is no account of engagement, self-efficacy or the cost of frequent checking on experience (the doc itself notes the rhythm "may prove unpleasant").
- **Domain scope.** The theory is best matched to derivable, well-structured domains (math, logic, physics). Arbitrary facts, skills and ill-structured domains need other mechanisms.

### Concrete suggested changes and open questions for the owner
1. **Replace or supplement the terminal condition.** Keep "feels obviously true" as an experienced target but add independent verification: re-derivation without hints, a near-transfer and a far-transfer item, and a delayed re-check (next session). Decide what the system does when the learner reports "obvious" but fails the check.
2. **Add a retention layer to the theory.** State that held knowledge is maintained by spaced retrieval, even of well-understood facts, and say where it goes (it can start as the "retrieval card" already in vision.md).
3. **Resolve the struggle/obviousness tension.** Either allow a short attempt-before-reveal at each foundation or key step (pretesting; productive failure-lite), or explain why struggle happens elsewhere (e.g., in the checks). Open question: where exactly does the learner's effortful generation occur in a lesson built so that every step is obvious?
4. **Make step size adaptive.** Define single-step relative to the learner's current chunking, allow larger steps (and fading to problems) as checks succeed, and drop the unconditional "never multi-step" rule or restate it as a novice default.
5. **Rewrite C15.** "Foundations are stated plainly, with their scope built in," not "unconditional." Add a rule for later revisiting oversimplified foundations (the vision doc's context-relative node model already supports this).
6. **Split "logistics" into extraneous vs constitutive.** Offload source-finding, fact-checking and formatting; keep (or at least allow) the learner's role in deciding relevance and in predicting the next step, since this is generation/metacognition.
7. **Qualify "nothing off the path."** Allow re-exposure of held material for retrieval, and contrasting cases that are not strictly on the shortest path when the goal is conceptual discrimination or transfer.
8. **Design checks that cannot be passed by echo.** Prefer production, prediction and self-explanation prompts over recognition; vary them over time. Decide on delayed variants. Open question: should the "check after every step" cadence be tested empirically against a "check after every few steps" variant on delayed retention?
9. **Treat "finite effort per session" as an engineering heuristic, not a psychological law,** and drop any language implying a depleting reservoir.
10. **Plan an evaluation.** Because the strongest claims (felt obviousness; single-step lock-in; minimal tree) are not directly testable from the literature, the theory is a good candidate for A/B tests against retention and transfer measures at 1 week.
